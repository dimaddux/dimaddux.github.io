Dylan Maddux

2024-11-06

Some research has suggested that food cooked in iron pots tends to contain more iron than food cooked in other types of pots. One such experiment focused on Ethiopian cooking, using three types of pots (aluminum, clay, and iron) and three common Ethiopian dishes. The investigators cooked four samples of each dish using each of the three pots, for a total of 3 x 3 = 9 treatment combinations and N = 36 observations. The 36 dishes were cooked in a randomized order, and the iron content of each dish was measured in milligrams of iron per 100 grams of cooked food. The investigators expected that the meat dish would contain the most iron, followed by the legume dish and then the vegetable dish, but they were interested in determining whether the iron content was affected by the type of pot, and whether the effect of pot type on iron content would depend on which food was being cooked.

Question 4 Obtain an interaction plot between pot type and food type and explain what it indicates about the questions of interest in this study. How does the effect of pot type depend on the type of food being cooked? For each type of food, what type(s) of pot appear to produce the greatest mean iron content?

ggplot(Cooking, aes(type, iron, color = food, group = food)) +

The effect of pot type on iron content is greatest for meat, while it is similar for legumes and vegetables. For all three types of food, iron pots appear to produce the greatest mean iron content, followed by clay pots then aluminum pots.

Question 6 Can any causal conclusions be drawn from this study? Do you think the conclusions from this study can be generalized?

Yes, since the variables were manipulated experimentally and the dishes were randomly assigned. However, the generalizability of the conclusions from this study are limited due to the small sample size and singular cooking source for all the dishes.
